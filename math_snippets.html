Projective and Injective Objects

I'm on my way to Tor and Ext. Seeing the Tor and Ext constructions on a homological algebra makes much more sense than it did back when I took algebraic topology. For instance I was not aware of the Hom-Tensor adjunction and, for some reason, my gut feeling back then was that the Tensor product was a simpler construction then the Hom contravariant functor (I don't really know why, maybe it is the contravariance). The Hom-Tensor adjunction makes the entire construction much more natural.

Also left and right exactness is SUPER confusing when thinking about functors as covariant/contravariant, because if $$0 \to X' \to X \to X'' \to 0$$ ia SES of, for instance, abelian groups, then if $G$ is any group both $$\operatorname{Hom}(X, G) \leftarrow \operatorname{Hom}(X, G) \leftarrow \operatorname{Hom}(X''', G) \leftarrow 0$$ and $$X' \otimes G \to X \otimes G \to X'' \otimes G \to 0$$ are exact So the $0$ remains on the right but the $(-) \otimes G$ functor is right exact while the $\operatorname{Hom}(-, G)$ functor is left exact. It makes so much more sense seeing this through the lens of the preservation of limits/colimits of each coordinate of the $\operatorname{Hom}(-, -)$ bifunctor. we can pull a colimit out of the first coordinate and a limit out of the second coordinate (both as limits). The fact that we can pull the colimit (a limit on the opposite category) out of the first coordinate makes the $\operatorname{Hom}(-,G)$ functor preserve limits, hence it is LEFT exact. By the adjunction then the tensor functor is right exact. This is still confusing to be honest, but it is much better now then when I looked at SES's exclusively.

At first, when learning about projective and injective objects I simply could not remember which way the arrows where supposed to point to. Which, in homological algebra, seems to be THE telltale sign that I don't understand what is going on. Currently I'm remembering the directions of the arrows because a projective object satisfies the same universal property a covering space does (and that they point the other way in the injective case). That is obviously horrendous so I got Gemini-Argon to make a PDF explaining projectivity and injectivity in the topological context with somewhat decent depth (also clearing this left-right exactness confusion of mine). I only skimmed the document but I'm very impressed with the model's capabilities.

The Hom complex

I just learned of a construction called $\mathrm{Hom}^*(X,Y)$ complex for $X,Y$ complexes over a complete abelian category (completeness for the product below to exist). It captures a disgusting amount of information about the maps between the complexes $X$ and $Y$. We define $$\mathrm{Hom}^n(X,Y) = \prod_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} \mathrm{Hom}(X^k, Y^{k + n})$$ And the differential $D$ here is given by the universal property of products using the map $D^k(\prod\limits_{k^\prime} f_{k^\prime}) = f^{k+1}d_X^k - (-1)^kd_Y^{n+k}f^k$. This admitedly is a bit of a hideous formula but we can ommit some indices if so desired. Anyway, the kernel of $D^0$ corresponds (not being overly precise here, but that isn't the goal anyway) to the chain maps $X \to Y$ and the image of $D^{-1}$ corresponds to the null homotopic maps, so that $\mathrm{H}^0(\mathrm{Hom}^*(X,Y))$ are the morphisms $X \to Y$ on the homotopy category of $C(\mathcal(A))$, which we denote $K(\mathcal{A})$. I'm not entirely sure of the interpretations of the other cohomology groups but there is a somewhat easy to prove formula that works under the usual sign conventions for the shifted complexes: $$\mathrm{H}^n(\mathrm{Hom}^*(X,Y)) \cong \mathrm{Hom}_{K(\mathcal{A})}(X, Y[n])$$ I'm pretty sure that the kernel of $D^{-1}$ corresponds to homotopies from maps to themselves and the image of $D^{-2}$ makes no sense to me currently (I have not thought about for too long). Anyway, this Hom complex point of view seems useful.

Scheme language superseeds classical language

The fact that the Scheme language superseeds the classical language is essentially the following theorem:
Theorem Let $k$ be an algebraically closed field, $Var(k)$ be the category of varieties over $k$ and $Sch(k)$ be the category of Schemes over $k$ (I believe you can describe it as a comma category but explicitly it has as objects morphisms $\phi_S: S \to Spec k$ and as morphisms the morphisms $\psi: S \to S'$ such that $\phi_S'\psi = \phi_S$). Then there is a fully faithfull functor $t: Var(k) \to Sch(k)$ (which I will not describe, it takes a while and is the core of the proof).

Hartshorne additionally says this is a "natural" embedding but I found no way of making this naturality make any sense. I don't know of some structure here that isn't captured by saying that $t$ is categorical embedding (if you see such structure please send me an email).

Kan Extensions

I'm currently taking a homological algebra course under Wen Wei Li. The course starts with quite a good bit of category theory, much more than the bare minimum. Currently I'm getting used to working with Kan extensions. A theorem that I've not tried proving but is likely not be so hard (although possibly is somewhat tedius) is that Kan extensions can be computed pointwise via limits/colimits on complete/cocomplete categories. My first reaction when hearing of this theorem was that limits and colimits where likely the more central concept compared to Kan extensions. A user of Math Stack exchange said (I'm testing blockquotes):

As a category theorist I am inclined to agree that Kan extensions in general do not seem to be as important as their various special cases. The Kan extension functor, i.e. the left/right adjoints of the precomposition functor, shows up often enough that it is convenient to have a name for it, though.

— Math Stack Exchange User

Which agrees with my first reaction if limits/colimits are to be considered the most important particular cases of kan extensions, which I find likely.

There are cases where kan extensions exist on non complete/cocomplete categories so that the Kan extension can't be computed by pointwise limit/colimit. Also it seems like Kan extensions are overall easier to work with in more general contexts (like $\infty-$categories or something like that) so I'm more on the Kan extension side than of the limit/colimit side.

Module Endomorphisms and Nakayama's Lemma

Not much super interesting this week due to exam prep, but I'm fascinated by the following lemma because it confuses me:

Lemma. Let $M$ be a finitely generated $A$-module with $A$ commutative. If $I \triangleleft A$ is an ideal and $\phi: M \to M$ is an endomorphism such that $\phi(M) \subset IM$, then there is a monic polynomial relation: $$a_0 + a_1 \phi + \ldots + a_{n-1}\phi^{n-1} + \phi^n = 0$$ with $a_0, \ldots, a_{n-1} \in I$.

Its proof uses something called the adjugate matrix, which I had never seen before. The main point of this lemma is to prove Nakayama's Lemma:

Nakayama's Lemma. Let $M$ be a finitely generated $A$-module with $A$ commutative and $I \triangleleft A$ be an ideal contained in the Jacobson radical $J(A)$. Then $IM$ is a proper submodule of $M$ unless $M = 0$.

This lemma seems to appear everywhere, but I'm not yet aware of why. The statement feels somewhat arbitrary, but I'm sure I will realize its importance soon.

Completions & Local Behavior

I dabbled into Atiyah's construction of completions since I will need it for Hartshorne's Chapter I.5. Looking at the completion of $\mathcal{O}_{X,P}$ is how one studies the "very local behavior of $X$ near $P$." That distinction matters because studying open neighborhoods of $P$ does not isolate local geometry well:

Exercise. Say $X$ and $Y$ are varieties, $P \in X$ and $Q \in Y$. If $\mathcal{O}_{X,P} \simeq \mathcal{O}_{Y,Q}$, then there exist neighborhoods $U \ni P$ and $V \ni Q$ such that $U \simeq V$. In particular, $X$ and $Y$ are birationally equivalent.

Strict Transform of $Z(x^3 - xy - y^3)$

Let $Y = Z(x^3 - xy - y^3) \subset \mathbb{A}^2_{(x,y)}$. I was trying to understand the strict transform $\overline{Y} \subset \mathbb{A}^2_{(x,y)} \times \mathbb{P}^1_{(s:t)}$ and its intersection with the exceptional divisor $E$.

Computing explicit ideals via saturations is difficult by hand, so I tested candidates until reaching $Y' = Z(xt - ys,\, xs^2 + st + yt^2)$. We have $Y' \setminus E = \overline{Y} \setminus E$, meaning $Y' \supset \overline{Y}$, and $Y' \cap E$ consists of just two points.

Because $Y$ is defined by equations symmetric in $x$ and $y$, so is $\overline{Y}$. Since $((0,0), (1:1)) \notin Y' \supset \overline{Y}$, the set $E \cap \overline{Y}$ must have even cardinality if finite. Because it is bounded above by $|Y' \cap E| = 2$ and cannot be empty, $|E \cap \overline{Y}| = 2$, proving $\overline{Y} = Y'$.

Projective Closures and Saturation

For an affine variety $Y \subset \mathbb{A}^n$, describing $I(\overline{Y})$ is subtle. The naive homogenization of $I(Y)$ generates extraneous components at infinity and may fail to be prime. The clean characterization requires saturating the ideal:

Homogenize then Saturate. Let $Y \subset \mathbb{A}^n_{(x_1, \ldots, x_n)}$ be an affine variety and $\overline{Y} \subset \mathbb{P}^n_{(t:x_1, \ldots, x_n)}$ its projective closure. Let $H_t$ be the homogenization map: $$H_t(f) = t^{\deg(f)} f(x_1/t, \ldots, x_n/t)$$ Then: $$I(\overline{Y}) = \big(\langle H_t(I(Y)) \rangle : t^{\infty}\big) = \{f \in k[t, x_1, \ldots, x_n] \mid \exists m \ge 0,\; t^m f \in \langle H_t(I(Y)) \rangle\}$$

Krull's Hauptidealsatz

Working through Hartshorne I.1.9:

Exercise (Hartshorne I.1.9). If $I \triangleleft k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is generated by $r$ elements, then every irreducible component of $Z(I)$ has dimension at least $n - r$.

This led to conjecturing the general ring-theoretic form before checking the reference:

Theorem (Krull's Hauptidealsatz). Let $A$ be a Noetherian commutative ring and $J \triangleleft A$ be generated by $r$ elements. Then every minimal prime ideal containing $J$ has height at most $r$.
